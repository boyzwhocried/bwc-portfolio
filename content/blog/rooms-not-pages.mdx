a portfolio answers a straightforward question: what have you made?

that is a useful question, especially when someone needs to see the work. it is not the only question a personal site can answer.

## the difference is permission

a page is usually a category. projects go here. writing goes there. contact details live at the end.

a room has a mood and a reason to stay. it can hold a listening practice, a wall of photographs, a current note, or a small experiment that refuses to become a product. it gives the work some neighbours.

that does not make the site less serious. it makes the work easier to understand. systems do not appear from nowhere. they come from taste, curiosity, repetition, and the odd idea that was worth keeping around.

## a lobby, not a funnel

the homepage should not pressure every visitor into the same path. some people will arrive for a project. some will find a photograph first. some will only want to see what is being made right now.

the job of the front page is to orient them, then let them choose a door.

work still deserves a clear route. so do the quieter parts. the navigation can stay small when it groups the obvious paths and keeps the rest in a compact room index. not every room needs a permanent sign above the door.

## one place, many registers

the visual system stays consistent so the rooms feel related. the tone can still shift. one room can read like an index, another like a journal, another like a shelf of toys.

that balance is what makes a personal site worth revisiting. it has a recognizable address, but it does not make every part of a person wear the same uniform.
